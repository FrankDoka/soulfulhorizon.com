In a world that never slows down, the concept of rest can feel foreign — even indulgent. Many of us don’t slow down until exhaustion forces us to, pushing through chronic stress and calling it normal until burnout leaves no other choice. But rest isn’t laziness. It’s necessary for your mind, body, and soul. Especially for those navigating mental health challenges, rest is an act of healing, reflection, and spiritual connection.

## What Is Rest, Really?

Rest is more than just sleep. It’s emotional, mental, and spiritual restoration. It’s moments where you allow yourself to simply *be* — without striving, performing, or fixing.

- **Mental rest:** Stepping away from screens, multitasking, and overstimulation.
- **Emotional rest:** Letting go of people-pleasing, perfectionism, and inner criticism.
- **Spiritual rest:** Reconnecting with God through stillness, prayer, or simply breathing in His presence.

## Why Rest Can Feel So Hard

You already know rest matters. So why is it still so hard to do it?

For a lot of women, especially the ones who are used to being the reliable one (showing up for everyone, carrying responsibilities, caring for others, serving, working), rest doesn’t come easily. It can feel selfish. It can feel like something you have to earn before you’re allowed to have it. And when your responsibilities aren’t finished, sitting still can feel almost impossible.

- Guilt for not being productive
- Productivity tied to your worth, so slowing down can feel like losing value
- Fear of disappointing others if you're not available or dependable
- Faith mistaken for endless self-sacrifice, with no room left for your own needs
- Survival mode for so long that stillness itself feels unfamiliar, even uncomfortable

These patterns, often learned over time, can be gently unlearned.

It’s also worth naming the difference between rest and avoidance. Rest is intentional restoration. It’s not the same as disengaging indefinitely or using distraction to avoid something that still needs your attention.

## Why Rest Matters for Mental Health

Neglecting rest can contribute to burnout, anxiety, and depression. Rest is widely understood to support stress regulation, emotional resilience, and the mental space needed for clarity and creativity.

> “Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.” — Matthew 11:28

## Practical Ways to Rest Your Soul

You don’t need to do all of these. Start with one. A single small form of rest still counts.

- **Sabbath rhythms:** Set aside intentional time each week to rest and reflect. Turn off notifications, take a walk, or worship quietly.
- **Journaling without judgment:** Let your thoughts flow freely. This helps release inner tension.
- **Digital rest:** Step away from screens and notifications, even for a short stretch. Give your mind space that isn’t filled with input.

Rest can also help you notice your actual capacity: not how much more you can force yourself to carry, but what you genuinely have to give right now. Think of rest as a rhythm you return to, not a reward you have to earn.

## Faith Without Carrying It Alone

Prayer and spiritual rest can be deeply meaningful, and they don’t have to carry the weight by themselves. There’s room for therapy, medical care, boundaries, and community support alongside your faith. Rest doesn’t have to come from only one place.

## Biblical Reflections on Rest

In Genesis, God rests on the seventh day. One way to understand this is as a model of rest as holy, not only necessary.

> Psalm 23:2–3 (ESV) — “He makes me lie down in green pastures, He leads me beside still waters. He restores my soul...”

> Exodus 33:14 (ESV) — “...My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest.”

These verses point to something beyond permission to rest. For many, they read as a promise that God’s presence stays with us, even in our need for rest.

## Final Thoughts

Rest doesn’t mean stepping back from life. When you prioritize soul-level rest, you’re not falling behind. You’re making space to reconnect with God and with yourself. In stillness, we remember who we are, and whose we are.
