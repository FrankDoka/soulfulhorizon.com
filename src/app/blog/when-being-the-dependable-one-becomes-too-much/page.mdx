You know that person everyone calls when something needs to get done? The one who remembers the details, checks on everybody, volunteers to help, and somehow figures things out even when her own plate is already full?

Maybe you’re that person.

You take pride in being dependable. You genuinely enjoy helping others and being the one your family, friends, church, or community can rely on. And technically there’s nothing wrong with that.

But what happens when being dependable starts costing you more than you realize?

What happens when you’ve become so accustomed to taking care of everything and everyone that you no longer recognize when you need to care for yourself?

For many women, especially those who have grown up with strong expectations around responsibility, service, and caring for others, slowing down can feel uncomfortable. You may recognize that you’re exhausted but still struggle to give yourself permission to do anything about it.

You tell yourself you’ll rest when things settle down.

But how often does that actually happen?

## When Being Dependable Becomes an Expectation

One of the challenges with being the person everyone counts on is that people can become accustomed to your availability. Eventually, you may become accustomed to it, too.

You automatically say yes when someone needs help. You rearrange your schedule to accommodate everyone else’s priorities. You take on responsibilities because you know you’ll get them done, even when you technically don’t have the time.

And sometimes, nobody is even asking you to do all of these things anymore. You simply got used to anticipating what needs to be done and taking care of it before anyone else has to.

Over time, you may start noticing that you’re more irritable, less patient, or resentful of requests that wouldn’t usually bother you.

Perhaps you feel guilty when you finally have time to yourself because you’re thinking about everything else you could be doing.

Or maybe you’re struggling to enjoy activities you once looked forward to because everything has begun to feel like another chore.

These experiences don’t automatically mean you’re experiencing burnout or have a mental health issue. However, they can be signs that your current responsibilities are starting to exceed your capacity.

As a therapist, one of the things I’ve learned throughout my clinical work is how easily people can become accustomed to functioning under chronic stress. When you’ve spent years pushing through difficult circumstances, continuing to function can begin to feel like evidence that everything is manageable.

But being able to continue doesn’t necessarily mean what you’re doing is sustainable.

And being dependable shouldn’t require you to consistently neglect yourself.

## When Faith and Responsibility Become Intertwined

For Christian women, the relationship between service, responsibility, and rest can sometimes become complicated.

We are encouraged to love our neighbors, serve others, care for our families, and use our gifts to bless those around us. These are meaningful expressions of our faith.

However, depending on our upbringing, cultural experiences, and personal beliefs, we may begin interpreting these responsibilities in ways that leave little room for our own needs.

For Christian women who also identify as Afro-Caribbean, Latinx, and/or BIPOC, cultural beliefs around strength, resilience, and tenacity can reinforce those expectations.

You might feel selfish when you turn down an opportunity to serve, guilty when you need help, or uncomfortable setting boundaries because you’re worried about disappointing someone.

Perhaps you’ve even convinced yourself that being a good Christian means always being willing to do more.

But serving others and acknowledging your limitations are not mutually exclusive.

We are human. Our time, energy, attention, and emotional capacity are limited. Ignoring those limitations doesn’t make them disappear.

And when we continually push past them, we may find ourselves struggling to experience the very joy and peace that once accompanied the things we loved doing.

That brings me to a Bible verse I want us to consider.

## Making Room for God’s Comfort

> “When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul.” — Psalm 94:19 (ESV)

I love that this verse acknowledges something we sometimes struggle to admit: our hearts can carry many cares.

The psalmist didn’t pretend that his concerns didn’t exist. He recognized that they were numerous, while also recognizing that God’s comfort was available to him in the middle of those concerns.

There’s something important about that distinction.

We don’t have to pretend we’re okay or wait until every responsibility has been fulfilled before seeking comfort.

Sometimes we’re so focused on everything we’re responsible for that we barely give ourselves an opportunity to acknowledge what we’re carrying.

Our minds are occupied with the next appointment, the next project, the next person who needs something, or the next problem that needs to be solved.

And often when we do finally have an opportunity to rest, we’re still mentally preparing for whatever comes next.

And somewhere in the middle of all that activity, we may lose touch with the things that once brought us joy.

When was the last time you enjoyed a quiet moment without feeling obligated to make it productive?

When was the last time you spent time in prayer without mentally reviewing everything you needed to accomplish afterward?

When was the last time you did something you genuinely enjoyed simply because you enjoyed it?

God’s comfort isn’t something we earn by completing our responsibilities. And making room for it doesn’t mean we have to abandon everything we’re responsible for.

It means recognizing that we also need opportunities to receive, reflect, recover, and experience joy.

For some of us, that may require changing the way we’ve been living.

## Building Sustainable Rhythms That Make Room for Peace

If you’ve followed Soulful Horizon for a while, you’ve probably noticed that I talk a lot about sustainable rhythms.

That’s intentional.

I’m not particularly interested in encouraging women to build elaborate self-care routines that only work when they have an entire day to themselves and nothing unexpected happens.

I’m interested in what it looks like to create a way of living that acknowledges your actual responsibilities, your current capacity, and your need for rest and connection.

Because sometimes the problem isn’t that you need another self-care activity.

It’s that your current way of living leaves no room for one.

Building sustainable rhythms requires us to look beyond how we manage our time and begin examining what we’re asking ourselves to carry.

That might mean recognizing when you’re automatically agreeing to commitments without considering whether you have the capacity to fulfill them.

It may involve examining beliefs you’ve held for years about what it means to be strong, responsible, faithful, or dependable.

It could mean allowing someone else to take responsibility for something you would ordinarily handle yourself, and being okay with doing so.

And sometimes, it means accepting that certain commitments simply don’t fit your current season of life.

None of these changes necessarily eliminate stress. Life will continue to present responsibilities, challenges, and unexpected circumstances.

But they can help create room for the things that can help you navigate those stressful moments.

That might include more intentional time with God, meaningful relationships, adequate rest, activities you enjoy, or simply having enough space in your schedule that one unexpected event doesn’t derail your entire week.

Sometimes making room for peace begins with a very ordinary decision to stop treating every request as an obligation.

And sometimes it begins with recognizing that you need additional support. When stress becomes persistent, begins affecting your daily functioning, or brings unresolved emotional experiences to the surface, working with a mental health professional may be an important part of caring for yourself.

## What Are You Making Room For?

I’d like you to consider something this week.

Rather than immediately thinking about everything you need to accomplish, take a moment to think about what you’ve been missing.

Have you been so busy taking care of others that you haven’t had an opportunity to connect with God without rushing?

Are there relationships you want to nurture but never seem to have the energy and/or time for?

Have you stopped participating in activities that bring you joy because there’s always something more important to do?

Or perhaps you’ve been so focused on meeting expectations that you haven’t stopped to consider whether those expectations are realistic.

**What might change if you began making room for those things now instead of waiting until you’re completely exhausted?**

You don’t necessarily need to overhaul your entire schedule. Start by identifying one area where your current responsibilities are leaving little room for your own needs.

Then consider what you could realistically change.

Maybe it’s a commitment you need to reconsider, a boundary you’ve been avoiding, or an opportunity to ask someone else for help.

Perhaps it’s intentionally setting aside time to enjoy something without feeling obligated to accomplish anything.

Whatever it is, remember that creating sustainable rhythms isn’t about doing everything perfectly. It’s about developing patterns that support you through the different seasons of your life.

And as you begin making those changes, I encourage you to return to Psalm 94:19.

Your heart may be carrying many cares right now. That doesn’t mean you have to ignore them, carry them alone, or postpone receiving God’s comfort until life becomes less demanding.

There is still room for peace. There is still room for joy.

And you are allowed to make room for both while continuing to care about the people and responsibilities that matter to you.
